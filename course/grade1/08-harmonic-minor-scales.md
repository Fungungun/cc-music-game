# Harmonic Minor Scales

## What you will learn

- What makes a minor scale sound different from a major scale
- The harmonic minor scale formula
- The three Grade 1 harmonic minor scales: A, D and E

## AMEB Syllabus Reference

> **AMEB Piano 2026 · Grade 1 · Section III**
> Candidates must be able to name all notes of A harmonic minor, D harmonic minor and E harmonic minor scales. They must understand that the harmonic minor raises the 7th degree, and be able to identify the raised 7th note of any Grade 1 minor scale.

## Major vs Minor

You have already learned that the major scale pattern (T T S T T T S) gives a bright, confident character. **Minor scales** use a different pattern and have a darker, more expressive character.

There are different types of minor scale. The AMEB Piano exam requires the **harmonic minor** — this is the most important form because it is used in chords and cadences throughout classical music.

## The Natural Minor Scale

First, it helps to understand the natural minor scale. Its formula is:

**T – S – T – T – S – T – T**

A natural minor: A B C D E F G A

```abc
X:1
T:A natural minor scale
L:1/4
K:Am
A B c d e f g a |]
```

Listen to how this sounds compared to A major (A B C# D E F# G# A). The natural minor has a flatter, more melancholy sound.

## The Harmonic Minor Scale

The **harmonic minor** modifies the natural minor by **raising the 7th note by one semitone**. For A minor, this changes G to **G#**.

**Formula:** T – S – T – T – S – **A2** – S

(The interval between the 6th and 7th becomes an augmented 2nd — 3 semitones — giving the scale its distinctive "exotic" sound.)

> **Teaching tip:** Play A natural minor on the piano. Then play A harmonic minor. The raised 7th (G#) creates a strong pull toward the tonic (A) — this is called a **leading tone**. Ask your student: "Can you hear how the G# really wants to resolve up to A?" This leading tone is why harmonic minor is used in cadences.

## A Harmonic Minor

Starting on A, with the 7th note raised:

**Notes:** A B C D E F **G#** A

```abc
X:1
T:A harmonic minor scale
L:1/4
K:Am
A B c d e f ^g a |]
```

Notice: A harmonic minor uses the same notes as C major (C D E F G A B C) except for the raised G#. This is no coincidence — A minor is the **relative minor** of C major (more on this in the Key Signatures lesson).

## D Harmonic Minor

Starting on D, applying the harmonic minor pattern:
D→E (T), E→F (S), F→G (T), G→A (T), A→Bb (S), Bb→C# (A2), C#→D (S).

The 7th note (C) must be raised to **C#**. Also note: the 6th note is Bb (the same as in F major).

**Notes:** D E F G A Bb **C#** D

```abc
X:1
T:D harmonic minor scale
L:1/4
K:Dm
D E F G A _B ^c d |]
```

## E Harmonic Minor

Starting on E:
E→F# (T), F#→G (S), G→A (T), A→B (T), B→C (S), C→D# (A2), D#→E (S).

The 7th note (D) must be raised to **D#**. Also note: the 2nd note is F# (the same as in G major).

**Notes:** E F# G A B C **D#** E

```abc
X:1
T:E harmonic minor scale
L:1/4
K:Em
E ^F G A B c ^d e |]
```

## Summary of Grade 1 Harmonic Minor Scales

| Scale | Key accidentals | Raised 7th |
|---|---|---|
| A harmonic minor | none (same as C major) | G → G# |
| D harmonic minor | Bb (same as F major) | C → C# |
| E harmonic minor | F# (same as G major) | D → D# |

## Relative Minor

Each major scale has a **relative minor** — they share the same key signature (same sharps and flats). The relative minor starts on the **6th note** of the major scale (or equivalently, 3 semitones below the major tonic):

- C major → A harmonic minor
- G major → E harmonic minor
- F major → D harmonic minor

## How the examiner tests this

- "Name the notes of the E harmonic minor scale."
- "What is the raised 7th note in D harmonic minor?"
- "Write the A harmonic minor scale ascending on the bass clef."
- "What is the difference between A natural minor and A harmonic minor?"
- "What note is raised in the harmonic minor scale?"

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting to raise the 7th note.** This is the defining feature of harmonic minor. Natural minor without the raised 7th is a different scale.
- **Raising the wrong note.** Count up 7 notes from the tonic (including both ends) to find the 7th.
- **Confusing D harmonic minor and A harmonic minor.** D harmonic minor has a Bb (6th note) *and* a C# (raised 7th). A harmonic minor has neither a flat nor a sharp except for the raised G#.
- **Writing D# instead of Eb in E harmonic minor.** D# and Eb are enharmonically the same key, but D# is the correct spelling in E harmonic minor because you need a D of some kind as the 7th note (E F# G A B C D → raise D to D#).

## Key points

- **Harmonic minor** = natural minor with the **7th note raised by one semitone**.
- **A harmonic minor**: A B C D E F G# A — raised 7th is G#.
- **D harmonic minor**: D E F G A Bb C# D — has Bb and raised C#.
- **E harmonic minor**: E F# G A B C D# E — has F# and raised D#.
- Relative minor shares the key signature with its major: A minor ↔ C major, E minor ↔ G major, D minor ↔ F major.
- The raised 7th creates a **leading tone** that pulls strongly toward the tonic.
