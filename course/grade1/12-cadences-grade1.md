# Cadences — Perfect and Plagal

## What you will learn

- What a cadence is
- The Perfect cadence (V → I) — the "full stop"
- The Plagal cadence (IV → I) — the "Amen"
- How to identify cadences in written music and by ear

## AMEB Syllabus Reference

> **AMEB Piano 2026 · Grade 1 · Section III**
> Candidates must be able to identify the **Perfect cadence (V → I)** and **Plagal cadence (IV → I)** in C major — naming both chords, their Roman numerals, and the cadence type. Cadences may be presented written on the staff or played by the examiner.

## What Is a Cadence?

A **cadence** is a pair of chords that comes at the end of a musical phrase. It creates a sense of arrival, rest or pause — similar to punctuation in writing. Just as a sentence ends with a full stop, question mark or comma, a musical phrase ends with a cadence.

At Grade 1, you need to know two cadences, both in the key of C major.

> **Teaching tip:** Play a simple 8-bar melody and stop on beat 1 of bar 8. Ask: "Does that sound finished or unfinished?" This is exactly what a cadence controls — the sense of arrival or of leaving something unresolved.

## The Perfect Cadence — V → I

The **Perfect cadence** moves from chord **V** (dominant) to chord **I** (tonic). It is the most conclusive cadence in music — it sounds finished, resolved, like a full stop at the end of a sentence.

In C major:
- Chord V = **G major** (G B D)
- Chord I = **C major** (C E G)

```abc
X:1
T:Perfect cadence in C major — G then C
M:4/4
L:2
K:C
[GBd][ceg] |]
```

You will hear this cadence at the very end of nearly every piece of tonal music. It sounds like the music has "come home."

**How to remember it:** The Perfect cadence is perfect because it perfectly resolves — V always wants to move to I (the 7th note of chord V, the leading tone, pulls upward to the tonic).

## The Plagal Cadence — IV → I

The **Plagal cadence** moves from chord **IV** (subdominant) to chord **I** (tonic). It sounds resolved but softer than the Perfect cadence — more like a gentle sigh than a definitive full stop.

In C major:
- Chord IV = **F major** (F A C)
- Chord I = **C major** (C E G)

```abc
X:1
T:Plagal cadence in C major — F then C
M:4/4
L:2
K:C
[FAc][ceg] |]
```

The most famous example of a Plagal cadence: the word "Amen" sung at the end of a hymn. This is why the Plagal cadence is often called the **"Amen" cadence**.

**How to remember it:** Plagal → "Plainsong" → hymns → Amen.

## Comparing the Two Cadences

| Cadence | Chord sequence | Character | Common nickname |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect | V → I | Strong conclusion | "Full stop" |
| Plagal | IV → I | Gentle conclusion | "Amen" |

Both cadences end on chord I (the tonic chord). The difference is the chord that comes before.

## How to Identify a Cadence

When the examiner plays two chords or shows them on the staff:

1. **Identify both chords** (name the root and check whether the notes are from C, G or F triad).
2. **Determine the order** (which chord comes first, which second).
3. **Match to the pattern**: V → I = Perfect; IV → I = Plagal.

In C major (Grade 1 only):
- G major → C major = **Perfect**
- F major → C major = **Plagal**

## Chord Function Labels

Chords in a key are labelled with Roman numerals based on which scale degree they are built on:

| Chord | Roman numeral | Name | Notes (C major) |
|---|---|---|---|
| C major | I | Tonic | C E G |
| F major | IV | Subdominant | F A C |
| G major | V | Dominant | G B D |

The labels I, IV and V apply to any key — in G major, G major is chord I, C major is chord IV, D major is chord V.

## How the examiner tests this

- Play two chords → "Is this a Perfect or Plagal cadence?"
- Show two chords written on the staff → "Name this cadence."
- "In C major, what are the notes of chord V?"
- "What two chords make up a Perfect cadence in C major?"
- "What is another name for the Plagal cadence?"

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing the order.** Both cadences end on I. It is the *starting* chord that differs — IV starts Plagal, V starts Perfect.
- **Not naming both chords.** Always say both chords and the direction: "G major to C major — that is chord V to chord I, a Perfect cadence."
- **Saying "G to C" without explaining.** Use the Roman numeral labels where possible: "V to I." This shows you understand the harmonic function.

## Key points

- A **cadence** = two chords at the end of a musical phrase — musical punctuation.
- **Perfect cadence**: chord **V → I** (G major → C major in C) — sounds conclusive, like a full stop.
- **Plagal cadence**: chord **IV → I** (F major → C major in C) — sounds gentle, like "Amen."
- Both cadences end on chord **I** (the tonic).
- Grade 1: cadences are only tested in the key of **C major**.
