# Major Scales

## What you will learn

- What a scale is and why it matters
- Tones and semitones on the piano
- The major scale formula: T T S T T T S
- The three Grade 1 major scales: C, G and F

## AMEB Syllabus Reference

> **AMEB Piano 2026 · Grade 1 · Section III**
> Candidates must be able to name all notes of C major, G major and F major scales (ascending and descending). They may be asked to write a scale on the staff, identify the scale from its notes, or identify the key from a given set of accidentals.

## What Is a Scale?

A **scale** is a sequence of notes in stepwise order, from a starting note (called the **tonic** or **root**) up or down to the same note an octave away. Scales form the foundation of melody and harmony — almost all tunes and chords are built from the notes of a particular scale.

The word "scale" comes from the Latin *scala*, meaning ladder — you climb up note by note.

## Tones and Semitones

Two key measurements:

- **Semitone**: the smallest interval in Western music — one key to the right or left on the piano (e.g., C to C#, E to F).
- **Tone**: two semitones — skipping one key (e.g., C to D, G to A).

The pattern of tones (T) and semitones (S) is what gives a scale its character — major scales sound bright and confident; minor scales sound darker.

## The Major Scale Formula

Every major scale follows exactly the same pattern of tones and semitones:

**T – T – S – T – T – T – S**

This pattern is fixed. If you start on *any* note and apply this formula, you get a major scale.

> **Teaching tip:** Walk through the formula together on a piano. Count the keys: from C, move up a tone (skip one: C → D), another tone (D → E), then a semitone (E → F — no black key between them!), then tone (F → G), tone (G → A), tone (A → B), semitone (B → C — no black key). You have arrived back on C, one octave higher.

## C Major Scale

C major starts on C and uses no sharps or flats — all white keys. This makes it the easiest scale to see the pattern.

**Notes:** C D E F G A B C

```abc
X:1
T:C major scale
L:1/4
K:C
c d e f g a b c' |]
```

Pattern check: C→D (T), D→E (T), E→F (S), F→G (T), G→A (T), A→B (T), B→C (S). ✓

## G Major Scale

G major starts on G. Applying the formula:
G→A (T), A→B (T), B→C (S), C→D (T), D→E (T), E→F# (T), F#→G (S).

The formula demands a tone between the 6th and 7th notes (E to the 7th). E to F is only a semitone, so we must raise F to **F#** to make a full tone. This is why G major has **one sharp: F#**.

**Notes:** G A B C D E F# G

```abc
X:1
T:G major scale — note the F sharp
L:1/4
K:G
G A B c d e ^f g |]
```

## F Major Scale

F major starts on F. Applying the formula:
F→G (T), G→A (T), A→Bb (S), Bb→C (T), C→D (T), D→E (T), E→F (S).

The formula demands a semitone between the 3rd and 4th notes (A to the 4th). A to B is a tone, which is too large. We must lower B to **Bb** to make a semitone. This is why F major has **one flat: Bb**.

**Notes:** F G A Bb C D E F

```abc
X:1
T:F major scale — note the B flat
L:1/4
K:F
F G A _B c d e f |]
```

## Summary of Grade 1 Major Scales

| Scale | Sharps/Flats | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| C major | None | C D E F G A B C |
| G major | F# | G A B C D E F# G |
| F major | Bb | F G A Bb C D E F |

## The Tonic Note

The note a scale starts and ends on is called the **tonic**. The tonic gives the scale its name. In C major, C is the tonic. In G major, G is the tonic.

## Scales in Both Directions

The AMEB exam may ask you to identify or write a scale ascending (going up) or descending (going down). Descending simply reverses the note order. The sharps and flats remain the same going down.

## How the examiner tests this

- "Name the notes of the G major scale."
- "What sharp/flat does F major have?"
- "Write the C major scale ascending on the treble clef."
- "Build the G major scale on the piano keys shown." (Scale Builder game)
- "How many semitones are between the 3rd and 4th notes of a major scale?" (Answer: 1 semitone)

## Common mistakes

- **Playing F instead of F# in G major.** The most common error. Always apply the formula — E to F is only a semitone, but the formula needs a tone.
- **Playing B instead of Bb in F major.** A to B is a tone; the formula needs a semitone there, so B must be flattened.
- **Thinking the pattern changes for different scales.** It never does — T T S T T T S is always the same.
- **Starting or ending on the wrong note.** The scale must begin and end on the tonic.

## Key points

- A **major scale** is a sequence of 8 notes using the pattern **T T S T T T S**.
- **C major**: no sharps or flats.
- **G major**: one sharp — **F#** (the 7th note must be raised).
- **F major**: one flat — **Bb** (the 4th note must be lowered).
- The starting and ending note is called the **tonic**.
- E to F and B to C are always semitones (no black key between them).
