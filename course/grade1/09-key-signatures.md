# Key Signatures

## What you will learn

- What a key signature is and why it exists
- The order in which sharps and flats appear
- How to identify the key from its signature
- The relative minor for each Grade 1 key signature

## AMEB Syllabus Reference

> **AMEB Piano 2026 · Grade 1 · Section III**
> Candidates must be able to identify the key signature of C major (no accidentals), G major (1 sharp: F#) and F major (1 flat: Bb). They must also name the **relative minor** of each key, and be able to write any of these key signatures on the treble or bass clef.

## What Is a Key Signature?

Imagine writing a piece in G major. Every F would need a sharp sign. Rather than writing ♯ before every single F throughout the piece, musicians place the sharp *once* at the beginning of every line of music, between the clef and the time signature. This is called the **key signature**.

A key signature tells the player: "Every time you see this note, add the sharp or flat shown here — in every bar, in every octave."

```abc
X:1
T:G major — key signature shows F sharp
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:G
G A B c | d e f g |]
```

## The Order of Sharps

Sharps always appear in the same fixed order, regardless of which key you are in:

**F C G D A E B**

Memory sentence: **F**ather **C**harles **G**oes **D**own **A**nd **E**nds **B**attle.

- 1 sharp = F#  (G major)
- 2 sharps = F#, C#  (D major)
- 3 sharps = F#, C#, G#  (A major)
- And so on...

Each time you add one more sharp, it is always the next one in the sequence: F, then C, then G...

## The Order of Flats

Flats always appear in the reverse order:

**B E A D G C F**

Memory sentence: **B**attle **E**nds **A**nd **D**own **G**oes **C**harles **F**ather. (Or simply: the reverse of the sharps sentence.)

- 1 flat = Bb  (F major)
- 2 flats = Bb, Eb  (Bb major)
- 3 flats = Bb, Eb, Ab  (Eb major)
- And so on...

> **Teaching tip:** Notice that the sharps sentence and flats sentence are exact reverses of each other. If your student already knows one, the other is simply reversed. This is a useful connection to make.

## How to Identify the Key

**From sharps:** Look at the *last* (rightmost) sharp. Go up one semitone. That is the major key.
- Last sharp = F# → up one semitone = G → **G major**

**From flats:** Look at the *second-to-last* flat. That flat names the major key.
- Two flats = Bb, Eb → second-to-last = Bb → **Bb major**
- Special case: one flat (Bb only) → the key is **F major** (you simply memorise this one)

At Grade 1, you only need to know C major (0 accidentals), G major (1 sharp: F#), and F major (1 flat: Bb).

## Relative Minor

Every major key has a **relative minor** that shares the same key signature. They use the same sharps or flats but start on a different note.

The relative minor starts on the **6th degree** of the major scale — or equivalently, **a minor third (3 semitones) below** the major tonic.

| Major key | Key signature | Relative minor |
|---|---|---|
| C major | 0 accidentals | A minor |
| G major | 1 sharp (F#) | E minor |
| F major | 1 flat (Bb) | D minor |

The AMEB exam may ask: "What is the relative minor of G major?" Answer: "E minor."

Or the reverse: "What major key has the same key signature as D minor?" Answer: "F major."

## Key Signature Position on the Staff

Key signatures appear in a fixed position on each clef — the sharps and flats are always placed on the same lines and spaces, in the same order. You do not need to write accidentals in different octaves; the key signature applies to that note name in *all* octaves.

## How the examiner tests this

- Show a key signature → "Name the major key." (e.g., one sharp → G major)
- Show a key signature → "Name the relative minor." (e.g., one sharp → E minor)
- "How many sharps does G major have? What are they?"
- "Write the key signature for F major on the treble clef."
- "What key signature does A minor share?"

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting that 0 accidentals = C major, not "no key."** C major is still a key — it just has no sharps or flats.
- **Getting the one-flat rule wrong.** One flat = Bb. But the key is F major, not Bb major. This is the one case where the shortcut ("second-to-last flat") doesn't apply — you must memorise F major = 1 flat.
- **Confusing the key signature with the scale.** A key signature tells you which notes are sharpened or flattened. The scale starts on the tonic and uses those accidentals.

## Key points

- A key signature saves writing accidentals individually throughout the piece.
- Sharps appear in order: **F C G D A E B**.
- Flats appear in order: **B E A D G C F** (reverse of sharps).
- **C major**: no sharps or flats. **G major**: 1 sharp (F#). **F major**: 1 flat (Bb).
- Identify the major key: sharp keys → last sharp + 1 semitone; flat keys → second-to-last flat name.
- Relative minor shares the key signature but starts 3 semitones below the major tonic.
