# The Music Staff and Clefs

## What you will learn

- What a music staff is and how it works
- The treble clef and what it tells us
- The bass clef and what it tells us
- How the two clefs work together on the piano

## AMEB Syllabus Reference

> **AMEB Piano 2026 · Grade 1 · Section III**
> Candidates must be able to identify the treble and bass clef, explain the purpose of each, and locate middle C on both staves.

## The Music Staff

Written music is set out on a **staff** (also called a **stave**). A staff has five horizontal lines and four spaces between them. Each line and each space represents a different pitch — the higher up the page a note sits, the higher it sounds.

```abc
X:1
T:The Staff — five lines, four spaces
L:1/4
K:C
c4 |]
```

The lines are numbered from the bottom upwards: line 1 is at the bottom, line 5 is at the top.

## The Treble Clef

A **clef** is the symbol at the very left of every line of music. It tells you which pitches are written on which lines and spaces.

The **treble clef** (also called the G clef) has a large curling shape that wraps around the second line from the bottom. That line is always **G above middle C**. The treble clef is used for the upper part of the piano — the right hand, and for higher-pitched instruments.

```abc
X:1
T:Treble Clef — middle C to C above
L:1/4
K:C
c d e f g a b c' |]
```

## The Bass Clef

The **bass clef** (also called the F clef) is used for the lower part of the piano — the left hand, and for lower-pitched instruments. It has two dots on either side of the fourth line from the bottom, which is always **F below middle C**.

```abc
X:1
T:Bass Clef — C below middle C up to middle C
L:1/4
K:C clef=bass
C D E F G A B c |]
```

## The Grand Staff

For piano music, the treble and bass clef staves are joined together to make a **grand staff**. A vertical line called a **brace** connects them on the left, and bar lines run through both staves at the same time.

> **Teaching tip:** Point out the grand staff in any piano piece your student is learning. Show how the right hand reads the treble clef and the left hand reads the bass clef. Ask: "Which hand plays the higher notes? Which plays the lower notes?"

## Middle C

**Middle C** is special — it sits exactly between the two staves. On the treble clef it appears one ledger line *below* the staff. On the bass clef it appears one ledger line *above* the staff. It is the same note on the piano either way.

A **ledger line** is a short extra line added above or below the staff when a note goes beyond the five main lines.

```abc
X:1
T:Middle C on the treble clef (one ledger line below)
L:1
K:C
c |]
```

```abc
X:1
T:Middle C on the bass clef (one ledger line above)
L:1
K:C clef=bass
c |]
```

## How the examiner tests this

In the AMEB exam, the examiner will point to a note written on the treble or bass clef staff and ask:
- "What note is this?"
- "On which line or space does this note sit?"
- "Is this treble clef or bass clef?"

They may also ask you to write a named note on a blank staff, or to identify whether a piece of music is in treble or bass clef.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing treble and bass clef notes.** The note G sits on the *second* line in treble clef, but the note G sits in the *second space* in bass clef. The clef symbol always tells you which is which.
- **Forgetting that higher on the page means higher in pitch.** If a note looks like it is sitting up high on the staff, it sounds higher than a note sitting near the bottom.
- **Miscounting ledger lines.** Count carefully — each ledger line and the space above or below it represents a different note.

## Key points

- The staff has five lines and four spaces; higher position = higher pitch.
- The treble clef wraps around G (second line); used for the right hand.
- The bass clef dots surround F (fourth line); used for the left hand.
- Middle C appears on a ledger line just below the treble staff and just above the bass staff.
- The grand staff joins both clefs for piano music.
