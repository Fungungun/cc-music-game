# Intervals as Scale Degrees (Grade 3)

## What you will learn

- The Grade 3 way of answering interval questions
- How to relate any note above the keynote to its scale degree
- Practice with all keys studied at Grades 1–3

## AMEB Syllabus Reference

> **AMEB Piano 2026 · Grade 3 · Section III**
> Candidates will be given a keynote and asked to name the interval above it as a **scale degree**: e.g., "In C major, this note is the ___rd of the scale." The answer format is "the 3rd" / "the 5th" etc. (quality names are not required in this format at Grade 3, though understanding quality helps).

## The Grade 3 Interval Format

At Grade 2, you named intervals with quality + number: "Major 3rd", "Perfect 5th".

At Grade 3, the question changes format. The examiner gives you:
1. A **keynote** (the tonic of a major scale)
2. A second note written above it

You must say: "That note is the **[number]th** of [key] major."

**Example:** Keynote = C. Note shown above = E.
- Count: C(1) D(2) E(3) → E is the **3rd** of C major.
- Answer: "E is the 3rd of C major."

This approach links intervals directly to the major scale — a more musical way of thinking about pitch relationships.

## The Scale Degree System

Every note in a major scale has a degree number and a traditional name:

| Degree | Name | Example (C major) |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | Tonic | C |
| 2nd | Supertonic | D |
| 3rd | Mediant | E |
| 4th | Subdominant | F |
| 5th | Dominant | G |
| 6th | Submediant | A |
| 7th | Leading note | B |
| 8th (octave) | Tonic (upper) | C |

You do not need to memorise all the names (except Tonic and Dominant, which appear in cadence descriptions), but knowing them helps you understand the logic of the scale.

> **Teaching tip:** Have your student play each degree of C major and call out its number: "1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8." Then play a note at random within the scale and ask: "Which degree is this?" This is exactly what the exam tests. Do this exercise in all seven keys studied at Grades 1–3.

## Working Through Examples

### Key of G major (G A B C D E F# G)

Keynote = G. Note shown = D.
- G(1) A(2) B(3) C(4) D(5) → D is the **5th** of G major.

Keynote = G. Note shown = B.
- G(1) A(2) B(3) → B is the **3rd** of G major.

Keynote = G. Note shown = F#.
- G(1) A(2) B(3) C(4) D(5) E(6) F#(7) → F# is the **7th** of G major.

### Key of D major (D E F# G A B C# D)

Keynote = D. Note shown = A.
- D(1) E(2) F#(3) G(4) A(5) → A is the **5th** of D major.

Keynote = D. Note shown = C#.
- D(1) E(2) F#(3) G(4) A(5) B(6) C#(7) → C# is the **7th** of D major.

### Key of F major (F G A Bb C D E F)

Keynote = F. Note shown = Bb.
- F(1) G(2) A(3) Bb(4) → Bb is the **4th** of F major.

## Connection to Intervals Learned at Grade 2

The scale degree *is* the interval number. If a note is the 5th of a major scale, it is a perfect 5th above the tonic. The Grade 3 format simply asks you to think in terms of the scale rather than counting semitones:

| Scale degree | Interval (Grade 2 name) |
|---|---|
| 2nd | Major 2nd |
| 3rd | Major 3rd |
| 4th | Perfect 4th |
| 5th | Perfect 5th |
| 6th | Major 6th |
| 7th | Major 7th |
| 8th | Perfect octave |

When all notes come from within the major scale (diatonic), the degrees and interval qualities line up exactly as above. Knowing this table means you already understand the answer — Grade 3 just phrases it differently.

## Accidentals and Notes Outside the Scale

Occasionally a note may be *chromatic* (raised or lowered from its scale position). In that case, you could say: "That note is not in [key] major — it would be a sharpened/flattened [degree]."

At Grade 3, exam questions typically only use diatonic notes (within the scale), so this situation is rare. If it occurs, name the nearest scale degree and add the accidental.

## How the examiner tests this

- "The keynote is G. What degree of G major is this note?" (Shows D on the staff) → "The 5th."
- "In D major, name the degree of this note." (Shows F#) → "The 3rd."
- "In Bb major, what is the 4th degree?" → "Eb."
- "The examiner plays G as a keynote, then plays D. What is the relationship?" → "D is the 5th of G major."
- Written question: "In the key of A major, mark the 6th degree." → Write F# on the staff.

## Common mistakes

- **Reverting to Grade 2 format.** If the question asks for the degree number, don't answer "Major 3rd" — answer "the 3rd." Read the question carefully.
- **Counting from the wrong note.** Always count from the *keynote* given, not from the bottom of the staff.
- **Forgetting accidentals in the scale.** In G major, the 7th degree is F# (not F). Know the scale before counting.
- **Confusing "scale degree" with "interval above."** They are the same thing, just phrased differently — the 5th degree of C major is G, which is also a perfect 5th above C.

## Key points

- Grade 3 format: given a keynote, name the upper note as a **scale degree** ("the 3rd", "the 5th", etc.)
- Count from the keynote upward, using the correct major scale for that key (including accidentals).
- The degree number is the same as the interval number — it is just expressed in scale terms.
- Know all seven Grade 1–3 major scales thoroughly to answer these questions confidently.
- For the exam: state your answer clearly: "That note is the **5th** of G major."
