# Musical Form — Binary and Ternary

## What you will learn

- What musical form means
- Binary form (AB) and how to recognise it
- Ternary form (ABA) and how to recognise it
- How cadences mark section boundaries

## AMEB Syllabus Reference

> **AMEB Piano 2026 · Grade 3 · Section III (General Knowledge)**
> Candidates must be able to identify whether a piece (or an excerpt) is in **Binary (AB) form** or **Ternary (ABA) form**, and explain their answer — typically by describing how many sections there are, how they contrast or repeat, and what cadences mark the section endings.

## What Is Musical Form?

**Form** is the large-scale structure of a piece of music — how its sections are organised and relate to each other. Just as a story has chapters, and a building has rooms, a piece of music has sections.

Sections are labelled with letters: the first section is **A**, a contrasting section is **B**, and if the first section returns, it is labelled **A** again.

Cadences are the clearest markers of section boundaries. The section usually ends with a strong cadence (often Perfect), while the midpoint may end with an Imperfect cadence (creating forward momentum).

## Binary Form — AB

**Binary form** has **two sections**: A and B. Usually:
- Section A: begins in the tonic key, ends with an Imperfect or modulating cadence (not fully at rest)
- Section B: contrasting material, usually ends with a Perfect cadence in the tonic key
- Both sections are usually repeated: ||: A :||: B :||

```
A section       B section
|—————————|     |—————————|
(tonic)         (contrast, then back to tonic)
```

Binary form is common in Baroque music: Bach's minuets, Handel's keyboard pieces, and many dance movements from the Baroque suite.

> **Teaching tip:** Play a short binary piece (e.g., Bach Minuet in G or any simple dance from the Anna Magdalena notebook). Ask: "Can you hear when the music changes character? When does it feel like it has 'arrived' somewhere new?" Mark A and B sections with hand signals.

### Characteristics of Binary Form

- Two distinct sections (A and B)
- A often ends without full resolution (Imperfect or dominant cadence)
- B provides contrast and returns to the tonic at the end
- Each section typically 4–8 bars
- Often with repeat signs for both sections

### Example: A Binary Piece Structure

```abc
X:1
T:Binary form — Section A (ending on V)
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:G
d2 B2 | A2 G2 | F2 D2 | G4 |]
```

```abc
X:1
T:Binary form — Section B (contrasting, ending on I)
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:G
g2 f2 | e2 d2 | c2 B2 | G4 |]
```

## Ternary Form — ABA

**Ternary form** has **three sections**: A, B and A (the first section returns). Structure:
- Section A: main theme in tonic key, ends with a Perfect cadence (complete close)
- Section B: contrasting theme — often a different key, mood or texture
- Section A (return): exact or near-exact repetition of the first A section

```
A section       B section       A section (return)
|—————————|     |—————————|     |—————————|
(complete)      (contrast)      (return — feels satisfying)
```

The return of A gives ternary form its satisfying, "rounded" quality — you feel you have journeyed and come home.

Ternary form is extremely common in 18th and 19th century music: many slow movements, songs with a contrasting bridge, the da capo aria in opera, and minuet+trio movements (where Minuet = A, Trio = B, Minuet da capo = A again).

### Characteristics of Ternary Form

- Three sections (A–B–A)
- Section A fully closes (Perfect cadence) before B begins
- Section B contrasts in key, mood, texture or all three
- Section A returns, usually identical or very similar to the opening

### How to Tell Ternary from Binary

The key question: **Does the opening material return?**

- If the piece ends with the same music as the opening → **Ternary** (ABA)
- If the piece ends with contrasting/new music → **Binary** (AB)

| Feature | Binary (AB) | Ternary (ABA) |
|---|---|---|
| Number of sections | 2 | 3 |
| Does opening return? | No | Yes |
| Typical ending | New material | Return of A |
| A section closes fully? | Often not | Usually yes |
| Common in | Baroque dances | Classical minuets, songs, slow movements |

## Identifying Form in the Exam

When the examiner plays a piece or asks about a score:

1. **Listen for the number of distinct ideas.** How many clearly different themes or textures are there?
2. **Listen for returns.** Does the opening come back later?
3. **Check the cadences at section boundaries.** Where does the music feel "finished"? Where does it feel "paused"?
4. **Name the form:** Binary (AB) or Ternary (ABA).
5. **Explain:** "The piece is in ternary form because the opening A section returns after a contrasting B section."

## How the examiner tests this

- "Is this piece in binary or ternary form? How do you know?" → State the form and give a reason (e.g., "The opening theme returns after the contrasting section — that makes it ternary/ABA form.")
- "How many sections does a ternary piece have?" → "Three: A, B, and A again."
- "At what cadence does section A of a binary piece typically end?" → "An Imperfect cadence — it feels unfinished, leading into section B."
- "What is the difference between binary and ternary form?" → See the table above.
- "In your AMEB piece, what form is the first movement?" (Candidates should be able to describe the form of their own pieces.)

## Common mistakes

- **Calling any three-section piece ternary.** The defining feature is the *return of A*. A–B–C (three different sections with no return) is not ternary form — it is through-composed.
- **Missing the repeat signs.** In binary form, ||: A :||: B :|| means both sections are played twice — but it is still only two sections (not four).
- **Confusing ternary with da capo.** D.C. al Fine is a notation instruction; ternary is a structural description. They often coincide (a D.C. returns to the A section), but the form name to use in the exam is "ternary."

## Key points

- **Binary (AB)**: two sections, no return of A — A ends unresolved, B concludes in tonic.
- **Ternary (ABA)**: three sections, A returns after contrasting B — A ends fully, B contrasts, A comes home.
- Listen for whether the opening material comes back → the single clearest indicator.
- Cadences at section endings: Imperfect (pause/question) often ends A in binary; Perfect (conclusion) ends A in ternary and ends the whole piece.
- Know the form of your own AMEB exam pieces — you may be asked about them directly.
