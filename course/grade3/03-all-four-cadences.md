# All Four Cadences (Grade 3)

## What you will learn

- Two new cadences: Imperfect (I → V) and Interrupted (V → VI)
- How all four cadences create different musical effects
- A systematic way to identify any cadence from a score or by ear

## AMEB Syllabus Reference

> **AMEB Piano 2026 · Grade 3 · Section III**
> Candidates must be able to identify all **four cadence types** by name and describe their character: **Perfect (V → I), Plagal (IV → I), Imperfect (I → V or IV → V), Interrupted (V → VI)**. Tested in the keys of **C major, G major and F major**.

## Recap of Cadences Known So Far

| Cadence | Chord sequence | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Perfect | V → I | Strong conclusion — "full stop" |
| Plagal | IV → I | Gentle conclusion — "Amen" |

Both known cadences end on chord **I** (tonic). The two new cadences do *not* end on chord I.

## The Imperfect Cadence — Ending on V

The **Imperfect cadence** ends on chord **V** (the dominant). Because chord V is unstable and wants to resolve, the music feels unfinished — like a question waiting for an answer.

Any cadence that ends on **V** is imperfect. The most common versions are:
- **I → V** (tonic to dominant)
- **IV → V** (subdominant to dominant)

```abc
X:1
T:Imperfect cadence in C major — I then V
M:4/4
L:2
K:C
[ceg][GBd] |]
```

Think of the Imperfect cadence as a **question mark** — the music pauses on an uncertain note, asking for more. It is typically used at the end of the *first* phrase of a melody, before the second phrase provides the answer (often ending with a Perfect cadence).

> **Teaching tip:** Hum or play "Twinkle Twinkle Little Star." The melody stops on G at the end of "twinkle twinkle little star" — that landing on V is an imperfect cadence. Then it continues and resolves — perfect cadence at the end.

## The Interrupted Cadence — The Surprise

The **Interrupted cadence** moves from chord **V** to chord **VI** (the submediant — a minor chord in a major key). The ear expects V to resolve to I (a perfect cadence), but instead it goes to VI — creating a feeling of surprise or interruption.

In C major:
- Chord V = G major (G B D) [same starting point as a perfect cadence...]
- Chord VI = A minor (A C E) [... but it goes *here* instead!]

```abc
X:1
T:Interrupted cadence in C major — V then VI
M:4/4
L:2
K:C
[GBd][Ace] |]
```

The effect is like expecting a full stop but getting an exclamation point instead — surprise, a sudden emotional shift, or a deliberate avoidance of closure.

## Chord VI in C, G and F Major

| Key | Chord VI | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| C major | A minor | A C E |
| G major | E minor | E G B |
| F major | D minor | D F A |

Chord VI is always a minor chord (built on the 6th degree of the major scale).

## All Four Cadences — Complete Summary

| Cadence | Formula | Character | Analogy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect | V → I | Strong, conclusive | Full stop |
| Plagal | IV → I | Gentle, devotional | "Amen" |
| Imperfect | ? → V | Unfinished, questioning | Question mark |
| Interrupted | V → VI | Surprise, avoidance of resolution | Exclamation mark / detour |

## How to Identify Any Cadence

**Step 1:** Identify the key (from key signature or context).

**Step 2:** Name both chords using Roman numerals.

**Step 3:** Match the pattern:
- Ends on I: if starts on V → Perfect; if starts on IV → Plagal
- Ends on V: **Imperfect**
- V → VI: **Interrupted**

**Example (C major):** Chords shown: A minor → C major.
- A minor in C major = chord VI. C major in C major = chord I.
- VI → I — this does not match any standard cadence pattern. (This is unlikely in an exam — all four standard cadences are described above.)

**Example (G major):** Chords shown: D major → E minor.
- D major in G major = chord V. E minor in G major = chord VI.
- V → VI = **Interrupted cadence**.

## Cadences in G Major

| Cadence | Chords | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Perfect | D major → G major | D F# A → G B D |
| Plagal | C major → G major | C E G → G B D |
| Imperfect | G major → D major | G B D → D F# A |
| Interrupted | D major → E minor | D F# A → E G B |

## Cadences in F Major

| Cadence | Chords | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Perfect | C major → F major | C E G → F A C |
| Plagal | Bb major → F major | Bb D F → F A C |
| Imperfect | F major → C major | F A C → C E G |
| Interrupted | C major → D minor | C E G → D F A |

## How the examiner tests this

- Play or show two chords → "Name this cadence." → Identify key, name chords, match pattern.
- "What is the difference between a Perfect and an Interrupted cadence?" → Both begin with chord V; Perfect resolves to I, Interrupted surprises by going to VI.
- "What chord does an Imperfect cadence always end on?" → "Chord V."
- "In G major, write an Interrupted cadence." → D major → E minor.
- "How does an Imperfect cadence feel compared to a Perfect cadence?" → "Imperfect sounds unfinished; Perfect sounds conclusive."

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing Imperfect and Interrupted.** Imperfect *ends* on V; Interrupted begins on V and goes *away* from I to VI. Remember: Interrupted = the expected Perfect cadence is "interrupted" and goes to VI instead.
- **Forgetting that Interrupted always ends on chord VI.** Chord VI is a *minor* chord in a major key (VI = submediant minor).
- **Not identifying the key first.** A minor is chord VI in C major but chord I in A minor — context is everything.

## Key points

- **Perfect**: V → I — strong conclusion. **Plagal**: IV → I — gentle conclusion.
- **Imperfect**: ends on V — unfinished, questioning.
- **Interrupted**: V → VI — surprise; expected resolution to I is "interrupted."
- All four cadence types tested in **C major, G major and F major** at Grade 3.
- Method: identify key → name chords as Roman numerals → match the pattern.
