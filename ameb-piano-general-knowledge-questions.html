Practise the questions behind each piano piece

General knowledge is not a list of answers to memorise in isolation. The useful preparation is being able to point to the student’s own score and explain its key, metre, structure, signs, terms and musical background.

This is an independent practice resource, not an official AMEB question paper. The examiner’s wording and the appropriate depth can vary with the grade, pathway and repertoire. Confirm current requirements in the official syllabus and with the student’s teacher.

Questions to ask with the score open

What is the full title, and who composed it?Say both clearly rather than recognising them only on the page.
What key or tonality is the piece in?Point to the key signature and any accidentals that support the answer.
What is the time signature?Explain the number of beats and the note value represented by each beat.
Where does the first complete bar begin?Check whether the piece starts with an anacrusis or upbeat.
How is the piece divided into sections or phrases?Use repeat signs, double bars, changes of material and cadences as evidence.
Is the form binary, ternary or another simple structure?Describe what repeats or contrasts instead of naming a form without evidence.
What does the opening tempo or character term mean?Connect the word to how the student actually plays.
Which dynamic markings appear, and where do they change?Point out gradual changes as well as fixed levels.
Which articulation signs appear?Find slurs, staccato, accents, tenuto or other relevant markings in context.
What do the unfamiliar symbols or abbreviations mean?Use only signs that genuinely appear in this score.
Can you find a repeated rhythmic pattern?Clap or count it and explain where it returns.
Can you find a sequence, imitation or repeated melodic idea?Compare the starting notes and interval pattern.
Where does the melody move between the hands?Discuss voicing only when it is relevant to the piece.
Where is the musical climax?Support the choice with pitch, dynamics, texture or harmony.
Can you identify a phrase ending or cadence point?At early grades, hearing and locating the close matters more than over-labelling it.
What instrument was this music written for, if known?Some pieces are arrangements; check the edition or teacher’s notes.
What period, style or country is associated with the composer?Keep biographical details accurate and relevant to the music.
Which two details would help another pianist perform this piece well?Finish by connecting knowledge to sound, not reciting facts.

How a parent can use this without teaching the lesson

Ask, then wait

Give the student time to find the evidence in the score. If they are unsure, circle the question for the next lesson rather than supplying a guessed answer.

Keep the teacher in charge

Use the exact pieces and terminology the teacher has prepared. The sheet is a conversation prompt, not a replacement syllabus or marking guide.
